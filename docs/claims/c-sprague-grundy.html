Section 4.3, paragraph 1Sprague–Grundy theorem; for NIM the Grundy value is the XOR of the heaps

External cited result A published result used as background or input; not proved in Lean here.

1 Paper text

The breadth of impartial games makes the Sprague–Grundy theorem remarkable: every finite terminating impartial position under normal play is equivalent to a single NIM heap of some size, called its nimber or Grundy value. Equivalence here means that replacing the position by that heap preserves the winning or losing status of its sum with any other such game. In a disjunctive sum, a move is made in exactly one component. The nimber of a sum is the bitwise XOR of the component nimbers; in particular, the nimber of a NIM board is the XOR of its heap sizes.

2 Explain this

What equivalence means. In a disjunctive sum, each turn changes exactly one component. Replacing a component by its equivalent NIM heap preserves the winning or losing status with every other normal-play impartial component. This makes NIM central to the theory of a very broad class of games, even though computing the equivalent heap size can be difficult.

The Sprague–Grundy theorem says that every finite impartial game position behaves, in sums of games, like a single NIM heap of some size (its nimber or Grundy value). For NIM itself, the Grundy value of a position is the XOR of its heap sizes.

Caveat: the theorem guarantees that a nimber exists, not that it is easy to compute (see Section 8.3).

3 How this is formalised

The general theorem is classical background and is not formalised. For NIM, the fact that matters (losing exactly when the XOR is zero) is checked as Theorem 4.4.
