Section 1, paragraph 2NIM rules, impartiality and why NIM is central

Informal interpretation Motivation, interpretation or scope; not a formal claim.

1 Paper text

In NIM, two players take turns removing any positive number of tokens from a single heap; the player taking the last token wins. It belongs to the broad class of impartial games: both players have the same legal moves from a given position. Many such board games can be thought of as games with shared pieces, whereas in partisan games, such as chess, legal moves depend on which player is to move. NIM is central to impartial-game theory because every finite terminating impartial position under the last-move-wins convention is equivalent, in sums of games, to a single NIM heap (Section 4).

2 Explain this

NIM has heaps of tokens. On each turn, remove at least one token from one heap; taking the last token wins. For example, (3,5,6)(3,5,6)(3,5,6) can become (3,4,6)(3,4,6)(3,4,6), but a single move cannot alter both the first and second heaps.

Impartial games allow the same moves to either player. Shared pieces are a useful intuition; the formal condition concerns legal moves, not ownership. Chess is partisan because the players move different colours. The striking link to NIM is explained in Section 4.3, paragraph 1: every finite terminating normal-play impartial position can be replaced, in sums, by one NIM heap. This is a structural statement, not an efficient method for solving every impartial game.

3 How this is formalised

This introduction combines the classical impartial-game definition, Definition 4.1, with the NIM rules, Definition 4.2, and the external Sprague–Grundy theorem, Section 4.3, paragraph 1. The Lean development checks NIM, rather than the general Sprague–Grundy theorem.
